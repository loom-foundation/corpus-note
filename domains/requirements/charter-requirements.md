The Requirements domain covers the engineering spine from want to proof: the stakeholder's need, the testable obligation that answers it, the committed design that satisfies the obligation, the verification standard that checks it, and the pre-build condition of acceptability agreed before any of it is made.
The domain owns the solution-free discipline of the problem side and the verifiability discipline of the obligation side.
Constraint stays a requirement's type rather than a kind of its own.

## Inclusion test

Does the artefact state what is wanted, what must be true, how it is built, how it is checked, or what would make it acceptable, for one system under development?
Then it belongs to Requirements.

## Boundaries

A statement about the structure of the system rather than about an obligation on it belongs to Architecture and Systems.
A statement about who is served and what is offered belongs to Product Management.
A statement about how the work is scheduled belongs to Delivery and Execution.
None of these domains is chartered in this corpus yet; each boundary stands as a claim awaiting its receiver.

## Origin

Informative: provenance the domain draws discipline from, never a normative reference.

- **ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148**: the international requirements-engineering standard, which fixes the stakeholder and system layers and the characteristics of a well-formed requirement.
- **The INCOSE Guide for Writing Requirements**: the canonical rule set for verifiable requirement statements, and the reason the domain's obligation side is disciplined rather than stylistic.
- **The INCOSE Systems Engineering Handbook**: the systems tradition that places requirements inside a whole lifecycle, with verification as a first-class peer rather than an afterthought.
- **The IREB CPRE syllabus, with Pohl's *Requirements Engineering***: the practitioner curriculum from elicitation through management, the domain as taught rather than specified.
- **BABOK v3 (IIBA)**: the business-analysis tradition's treatment of the same spine in enterprise vocabulary, which keeps the domain legible outside engineering.
